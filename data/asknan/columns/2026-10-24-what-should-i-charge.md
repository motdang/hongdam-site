Write a ladder of round prices once, then let orders move the price one rung at a time.

## Shoup priced parking by how many spaces sat empty

Donald Shoup, a UCLA planning professor, wrote *The High Cost of Free Parking* (2005, updated 2011) [1][2]. He proposes that cities charge fair market prices for curb parking and spend the revenue on services in the neighborhoods that generate it [1]. A 2003 ACCESS Magazine article states the test: lower the price when too many spaces are vacant, raise it when too few are [3].

San Francisco ran that test on its meters. From August 2011 its transport agency, the SFMTA, changed each block's hourly rate about every eight weeks by occupancy, the share of spaces holding a car [4].

| Occupancy | Next hourly rate |
|---|---|
| 80 to 100 percent | up $0.25 |
| 60 to 80 percent | unchanged |
| 30 to 60 percent | down $0.25 |
| under 30 percent | down $0.50 |

Rates were not allowed above $6.00 or below $0.25 an hour. Over the two-year pilot the agency made ten adjustments, and the average meter rate went from $2.69 to $2.58 [4].

## The money stays on the block

Old Pasadena, California, had no meters until 1993, and its merchants opposed them. The city offered to spend all the meter revenue in Old Pasadena, and the merchants and property owners agreed [3]. In the 2001 fiscal year the district's 690 meters brought in $1.2 million after costs, and $448,000 of it repaid a $5 million loan for sidewalks and alleys [3].

## A ladder in baht

A ladder is a list of prices that are allowed. The lowest is the floor, the highest is the ceiling. The price sits on one rung. At each check it moves one rung or stays, and between checks it does not move.

An example made up for this column: a service with ten places a month, a ladder of ฿1,000, ฿1,200, ฿1,500, ฿2,000 and ฿2,500, and an opening price of ฿1,500.

| Orders last month | Share of the ten places | Price next month, from ฿1,500 |
|---|---|---|
| 9 or 10 | over 80 percent | ฿2,000 |
| 6 to 8 | 60 to 80 percent | ฿1,500 |
| 0 to 5 | under 60 percent | ฿1,200 |

From ฿2,500 the price cannot rise, and from ฿1,000 it cannot fall. Each step on this ladder changes the price by 20 to 33 percent.

A Thai ladder can carry nines. The Thailand Foundation says Thai numerology counts nine, เก้า (kao), as the luckiest number, and ties its sound to ก้าว (kao), "to move forward" [7].

## Hongdam runs its prices on this rule

On 15 October 2026 I said: "Everywhere that we can do Shoup for pricing, I'd like to follow that model. It's a universal preference for me." For Hongdam I put it as "prices set by the market itself. Round or auspicious numbers, generous on both sides. Rarely determined by me."

The shop's price list states the rule [5]. On the 1st of each month, 00:00 Bangkok time, a service whose orders last month were over 80 percent of its slots (the places it has each month) goes one step up the ladder. Under 60 percent, one step down. In between, no change. Each service stays between its own floor and ceiling. The list shows 1 December 2026 as the first check [5].

On a service card a reader sees a row of bars, one bar for each rung from the low price to the high price, with the current rung marked. Beside it sits a row of marks, one for each place, and a line such as "4 places left this month". Opened, the card reads "Price now", "low", "high" and "Prices move with demand on the 1st of each month" [6]. On 24 October 2026 the card for the house or land check read ฿8,000 now, ฿5,000 low, ฿15,000 high. Its six bars are the six rungs ฿5,000, ฿6,000, ฿8,000, ฿10,000, ฿12,000 and ฿15,000, taken from a single ladder of 36 numbers that runs from ฿10 to ฿200,000 [6].

Floors and ceilings differ by service. On the same day "Paper forms typed into a sheet" listed ten slots, a floor of ฿500, a ceiling of ฿2,000 and a price of ฿1,000 [5]. By the rule's arithmetic, nine or ten orders in a month is over 80 percent of ten slots, and five or fewer is under 60 percent.
